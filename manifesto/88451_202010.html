WarningHow to read this page

The scores are holistic judgements. Each model read the whole manifesto text (in chunks of up to 20,000 tokens) and rated every dimension from the text as a whole. The passages quoted further down are examples the model cited, not the basis of the score: a score that rests on two paragraphs cannot be reduced to a few words, and a quoted passage alone will often look too weak, or too strong, for the number next to it. Read the models’ own explanations first; they state the reasoning behind each block of scores. The quotes then show where in the text the models looked.

Populism, populist ideology and liberalism

Thirteen dimensions scored on 0–10 by three model families from the full manifesto text, each with a verbatim evidence quote. Scores are aggregated as in Rode & Stöckl (2026): a chunk counts if the model marks its evidence as verified and the quote is found verbatim in the manifesto chunk (and is not copied from the prompt); confidence-weighted mean within model, plain mean across models.

chunk 2 · The manifesto contains only incidental populist elements — a brief reference to universities being dragged into political games and a Solzhenitsyn-paraphrased good-vs-evil framing — but the dominant register is technocratic and policy-oriented, with no sustained people-vs-elite narrative.

chunk 3 · The manifesto shows minimal populist features: it mildly criticises institutional inefficiency and bureaucratic inertia, invokes citizens and businesses as beneficiaries, and warns vaguely about those who would restrict freedoms, but there is no sustained elite-vs-people framing, no Manichean moral struggle, and no unified "people" rhetoric.

chunk 4 · The text shows minimal populist features: there is a brief criticism of ruling parties for pre-election handouts and a passing appeal to citizens' dignity, but no sustained anti-elite framing, no moralized good-vs-evil narrative, and no invocation of a unified virtuous 'people' against corrupt elites.

chunk 5 · The manifesto (Laisvės partija, Lithuania 2020) is a technocratic, policy-oriented programme with minimal populist rhetoric; the closest it comes to anti-elitism is criticising politically motivated decisions rather than a corrupt elite, and it invokes "the people" only incidentally in aspirational framing rather than as a unified virtuous bloc against an enemy.

chunk 6 · The text is a detailed foreign and defence policy manifesto with minimal populist framing: elites are criticised only in the context of foreign authoritarian regimes (Russia's "kleptocratic regime"), not domestic elites; the people are invoked only briefly and without a strong us-vs-them domestic narrative; and moral framing is directed at geopolitical adversaries rather than internal enemies.

chunk 7 · The text contains only mild populist undertones — bureaucratic dysfunction and corruption are criticised, but there is no sustained people-vs-elite framing, no Manichean narrative, and no invocation of a unified virtuous people against a corrupt establishment.

Populist ideology

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto is explicitly and self-consciously a classical-liberal programme; the faint anti-establishment critique targets bureaucracy and political conformism rather than economic elites or national-identity threats, placing it marginally in centrist-populist territory with negligible left or right populist flavour.

chunk 2 · The manifesto is clearly liberal-centrist (Laisvės partija), advocating market deregulation, talent attraction, and reduced state intervention, with no meaningful left-populist redistribution or right-populist nativist content.

chunk 3 · The manifesto is primarily a technocratic, liberal-reformist programme with no strong populist ideological flavour; it combines pro-market economic liberalism with progressive social rights, placing it closer to centrist-liberal than left- or right-populist territory.

chunk 4 · The text does not fit clearly into left or right populist ideology; it combines market-liberal social policy (targeted welfare, pension savings), environmental reform, and rule-of-law concerns without strong redistributive or nativist populist framing, placing it at most weakly centrist-populist.

chunk 5 · The manifesto is predominantly a liberal-reformist programme focused on market efficiency, individual freedom, and rule-based governance; it contains a minimal solidarity element (solidarity pension fund) but no strong left-redistributive or right-nativist populist ideology, placing it very low on all populist ideology sub-dimensions.

chunk 6 · The manifesto is not meaningfully populist in ideological terms; it is a pro-EU, pro-NATO, liberal-democratic foreign and defence policy programme. Minor right-leaning elements appear in security/sovereignty concerns (restricting Chinese investment, energy independence from Russia), but these are framed in liberal-democratic rather than nativist terms, and there is no significant left-populist redistribution rhetoric.

chunk 7 · The populist flavour is minimal and does not align clearly with left or right populism; the text is a technocratic, pro-market reform programme (Laisvės partija / Freedom Party) with centrist-liberal orientation rather than a populist ideological project.

Liberalism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto is explicitly grounded in classical liberal values: it champions individual freedom, civil and LGBT rights (partnership legalisation, transgender rights), free-market and low-tax economic policy (no new taxes, lower income tax, business-friendly environment), and proposes listing state enterprises on the stock exchange, all pointing to a strongly pro-liberal stance across all four dimensions.

chunk 2 · The manifesto is strongly liberal across all four dimensions: it explicitly champions democracy and human rights, opposes discrimination, calls for drastic reduction of state intervention in markets, and proposes investment schemes and non-banking capital access to enable market-based finance.

chunk 3 · The manifesto is strongly liberal across all four dimensions: it champions rule of law, media freedom, and democracy; advocates extensively for LGBT+, minority, gender, and migrant rights; promotes free markets, deregulation, and tax reduction; and supports minority shareholder protections and transparent corporate governance enabling market function.

chunk 4 · The manifesto is strongly liberal across political and social dimensions, explicitly defending freedom of speech, press independence, religious equality and non-discrimination, and privacy rights, while also advocating flexible labour regulation and market-oriented environmental policy; no financial-market content is present.

chunk 5 · The manifesto consistently advocates for liberal democracy, individual rights, anti-discrimination, privatisation of public services, market competition in healthcare and employment, and support for venture capital and private enterprise, placing it firmly in the classical-liberal tradition across all four sub-dimensions.

chunk 6 · The manifesto is strongly liberal across all four dimensions: it explicitly champions liberal democracy, rule of law, and checks on power (lib_political); actively supports LGBTQ+ rights and opposes discrimination (lib_social); advocates free trade agreements and open markets (lib_economic); and endorses European bond issuance and monetary-fiscal coordination as market-enabling instruments (lib_financial_market).

chunk 7 · The manifesto is strongly liberal across all four dimensions: it explicitly champions free-market competition in culture, listing of state enterprises on the stock exchange, open capital markets for pension funds, transparency and rule-of-law in governance, and inclusion of marginalised social groups in cultural life.
