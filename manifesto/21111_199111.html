WarningHow to read this page

The scores are holistic judgements. Each model read the whole manifesto text (in chunks of up to 20,000 tokens) and rated every dimension from the text as a whole. The passages quoted further down are examples the model cited, not the basis of the score: a score that rests on two paragraphs cannot be reduced to a few words, and a quoted passage alone will often look too weak, or too strong, for the number next to it. Read the models’ own explanations first; they state the reasoning behind each block of scores. The quotes then show where in the text the models looked.

Populism, populist ideology and liberalism

Thirteen dimensions scored on 0–10 by three model families from the full manifesto text, each with a verbatim evidence quote. Scores are aggregated as in Rode & Stöckl (2026): a chunk counts if the model marks its evidence as verified and the quote is found verbatim in the manifesto chunk (and is not copied from the prompt); confidence-weighted mean within model, plain mean across models.

What the models say about this manifesto

Each model’s own explanation of its scores, one per block of dimensions, written together with the scores.

Populism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The text criticises the governing coalition's "aveuglement politique" and the state's "complaisances traditionnelles à l'égard de son lobby bancaire," showing mild anti-elitism, but the document is primarily a detailed policy programme rather than a populist appeal to a virtuous people against corrupt elites, yielding low-to-moderate populism scores overall.

chunk 2 · The text is a detailed policy manifesto from ECOLO (Belgian Greens) focused on social rights, housing, health, culture, and early childhood policy; it contains minimal populist framing — mild criticism of broken promises on unemployment and of market logic in media, but no strong elite-vs-people dichotomy, no conspiracy rhetoric, and no moralized good-vs-evil struggle.

chunk 3 · The text contains mild populist undertones: it briefly criticises neoliberalism as an elite ideology imposed on schools, frames its ecological project as being "for and with the people," and notes a divorce between citizens and a political class of "uncontrolled mandarins," but these are incidental remarks within a detailed policy programme rather than a sustained populist narrative.

chunk 4 · The text is a detailed Green party policy manifesto focused on institutional reform, justice, asylum seekers, environment, and transport; it contains only mild, incidental criticism of political will and industrial polluters, with no sustained populist framing of 'the people' versus corrupt elites.

chunk 5 · The text is primarily a detailed policy manifesto (transport, land use, energy, water, waste, agriculture, defence) with minimal populist framing; there is mild criticism of public authorities favouring business interests over citizens and some people-centred democratic participation language, but no sustained elite-vs-people narrative or Manichean moral framing.

chunk 6 · The text shows mild populist tendencies: it occasionally frames powerful actors (IMF, World Bank, major powers) as self-serving rule-setters at the expense of weaker nations, and invokes civil society and 'people power' as a positive force, but the overall tone is policy-oriented and technocratic rather than strongly populist.

Populist ideology

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto explicitly proposes a "redistribution massive des emplois et des revenus," eco-taxation, social rights expansion, and critiques of corporate tax privileges, placing it clearly on the left of the ideological spectrum, while explicitly rejecting both pure market liberalism and state socialism in favour of an ecological third way.

chunk 2 · The manifesto is clearly left-leaning in its populist flavour: it advocates redistribution, expansion of social security, rights for the poor and marginalised, and public financing of culture and housing, with no nativist or anti-immigration framing; the mild people-centric appeals are generic rather than ideologically centrist.

chunk 3 · The manifesto leans mildly left-populist through its emphasis on public-sector refinancing, positive discrimination for disadvantaged groups, and opposition to neoliberalism, but it is primarily a green/ecological programme with no strong right-populist or purely centrist-populist framing.

chunk 4 · The manifesto is primarily a Green/ecologist programme with left-leaning elements (social rights for asylum seekers, public transport investment, environmental regulation, redistribution of public space) but no significant right-populist or purely centrist-populist content.

chunk 5 · The manifesto is primarily an ecological/green policy programme; what little populist ideology exists leans mildly left (redistribution to low-income households, criticism of agro-industrial interests, workers' rights) but is subordinate to environmental and technocratic policy proposals rather than a coherent left-populist narrative.

chunk 6 · The manifesto leans left-populist in its emphasis on debt cancellation, redistribution, criticism of structural adjustment programs, and solidarity with developing countries, but it is primarily an ecological/green program without strong class-based or nationalist populist framing.

Liberalism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The text acknowledges market efficiency ("l'économie de marché montre journellement sa capacité à innover") but advocates significant public intervention, eco-taxation, corporate tax reform, and social redistribution, producing a mixed liberal profile: moderate on political and social dimensions, mixed on economic and financial-market dimensions.

chunk 2 · The manifesto strongly supports rule of law, democratic rights, and access to justice (lib_political), advocates positive discrimination and inclusion of minorities and disabled persons (lib_social), and takes a mixed stance on economic liberalism — rejecting pure market logic in media and culture while accepting some market mechanisms alongside public intervention (lib_economic); financial markets are not discussed.

chunk 3 · The manifesto is broadly pro-liberal on political and social dimensions—advocating democratic reform, transparency, equal rights for immigrants, and minority inclusion—while being mixed on economic liberalism (opposing privatisation of profitable public enterprises but supporting tax incentives and market-compatible cultural financing) and only marginally touching financial markets through a reference to modern financial engineering for municipal debt.

chunk 4 · The manifesto strongly supports political rights (access to documents, judicial oversight, participatory procedures), social liberalism (non-discrimination by race/nationality for asylum seekers), and a mixed economic stance (market incentives via eco-taxes alongside regulatory interventions), with no discussion of financial markets.

chunk 5 · The manifesto strongly supports democratic institutions, civil liberties, and citizen participation (lib_political high); it references fundamental rights and freedoms (lib_social moderate); on economic liberalism it is mixed—accepting market pricing for agriculture beyond quotas but favouring heavy state regulation, eco-taxes, and public investment; financial-market content is minimal, limited to a subsidised low-interest loan proposal.

chunk 6 · The manifesto is strongly pro-liberal on political and social dimensions (democracy, minority rights, rule of law, human rights), mixed-to-skeptical on economic liberalism (critical of free trade dogma, GATT liberalization, and IMF conditionality), and mildly skeptical of unregulated financial flows while supporting reform of international financial institutions.

chunk 2 · The manifesto strongly supports political liberties, social rights including health and education, and economic redistribution, reflecting a pro-liberal stance in political, social, and economic dimensions, though financial market specifics are not addressed.

chunk 3 · The manifesto strongly supports liberal principles such as the right to education, social inclusion, and economic policies favoring investment incentives, demonstrating a pro-liberal stance in political, social, and economic dimensions.

chunk 4 · The manifesto explicitly supports political rights and rule of law in asylum procedures, social equality regardless of race or nationality, and economic liberalization principles such as European liberalization and fiscal policy reform, indicating a pro-liberal stance.

chunk 5 · The manifesto explicitly promotes political liberalism through citizen participation and decentralization, social liberalism via protection of architectural heritage and social urban development, and economic liberalism by advocating coherent territorial planning and administrative capacity, but lacks discussion on financial market liberalism.

chunk 6 · The manifesto strongly supports democratic transparency, human rights, rule of law, economic liberalization, and reforms in international financial institutions, indicating a pro-liberal stance overall.

Gemini

chunk 1 · The text supports democratic rule of law and fundamental liberties, but takes a highly interventionist stance on economic and financial markets, advocating for eco-taxes and state-directed contracts.

chunk 2 · The text strongly supports political and social rights within a democratic state of law, but advocates for market-restricting economic policies such as a fixed book price.

chunk 3 · The text strongly supports political transparency, democratic reforms, and minority rights, but opposes economic privatization and market-based public services.

chunk 4 · The text strongly supports political and social liberalism through transparency, human rights, and non-discrimination, while advocating for ecological market interventions and eco-taxes that regulate economic activities.

chunk 5 · The text strongly supports democratic institutions and fundamental rights, but advocates for heavy state intervention, eco-taxes, and regulations that restrict free-market economic dynamics.

chunk 6 · The text strongly supports political democracy and human rights, but is highly critical of free-market economic dogmas like unrestricted free trade.
