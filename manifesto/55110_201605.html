WarningHow to read this page

The scores are holistic judgements. Each model read the whole manifesto text (in chunks of up to 20,000 tokens) and rated every dimension from the text as a whole. The passages quoted further down are examples the model cited, not the basis of the score: a score that rests on two paragraphs cannot be reduced to a few words, and a quoted passage alone will often look too weak, or too strong, for the number next to it. Read the models’ own explanations first; they state the reasoning behind each block of scores. The quotes then show where in the text the models looked.

Populism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto shows moderate populist features: it frames the existing development model as corrupt and backed by established political forces (anti-elitism), invokes the people's interests in parliament (people-centrism), and uses morally charged language about an "inhuman and predatory" model, but stops short of a full Manichean good-vs-evil narrative, focusing more on institutional reform than on a pure people-vs-elite struggle.

chunk 2 · The manifesto shows moderate populist features: it frames corruption and clientelism as systemic evils undermining society, invokes "the Cypriot People" whose assets must be protected from international banking "usurers," and uses moralized language ("ληστρική διάθεση των εξουσιαστών") against financial elites, but the overall tone is predominantly policy-programmatic rather than strongly populist.

chunk 3 · The text contains mild populist undertones — noting public distrust of political officials and a divide between privileged public-sector workers and the unemployed poor — but the overall tone is technocratic and policy-oriented (green economy, local governance reform, tourism restructuring), with no sustained elite-bashing or Manichean framing.

chunk 4 · The text shows moderate populism: justice is described as protecting only the wealthy few at the expense of ordinary citizens, all governing parties are blamed for Cyprus's failures since 1974, and there are calls for accountability of elites, but the tone is civic-reformist rather than strongly Manichean.

chunk 5 · The text is a Green/ecological party manifesto focused on energy, environment, education, and culture policy; it mildly criticises successive governments for failing to build proper infrastructure and perpetuating inadequate legislation, but does not frame politics as a Manichean people-vs-elite struggle, invoke 'the people' as a unified virtuous bloc, or employ strong populist rhetoric.

chunk 6 · The text contains mild populist elements: political leaders are criticised for serving narrow interests and the government is accused of favouring private monopolies, but these are issue-specific critiques rather than a sustained people-vs-elite narrative; people-centrism appears only incidentally in calls for universal health coverage.

chunk 7 · The text shows mild populist tendencies through occasional references to "ordinary citizens" versus political/business interests and criticism of partisan criteria, but the dominant focus is environmental and ecological policy rather than a sustained people-vs-elite narrative.

Populist ideology

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto leans mildly left-populist through its anti-globalization stance, opposition to TTIP as a vehicle of corporate power over democratic law, and concern for social equality and environmental justice, while also displaying right-leaning national-identity elements around the Cyprus occupation and foreign invaders; the overall ideological flavor is moderate and primarily green/ecologist.

chunk 2 · The manifesto leans left-populist: it explicitly rejects the liberal open-economy model promoted by the Troika, advocates state intervention for social solidarity, proposes a public bank to protect citizens from international financial institutions, and champions gender equality and workers' rights, with minimal right-wing nationalist content beyond a passing reference to the occupied homeland.

chunk 3 · The manifesto (Ecologist Movement – Citizens' Cooperation, Cyprus) leans mildly left-green: it rejects privatisation, advocates redistribution and fair taxation, and promotes green/sustainable development, but it is primarily an environmentalist-reformist platform rather than a strongly ideological populist one.

chunk 4 · The text blends mild left-leaning elements (worker protection, equal wages, anti-exploitation) with right-leaning restrictionist immigration positions (limits on migrant numbers, strict border controls, deportation), resulting in a mixed but slightly right-leaning populist ideological profile overall.

chunk 5 · The manifesto shows weak left-leaning tendencies through criticism of profit-maximisation and commodification, and mild preference for local over foreign expertise, but the dominant frame is ecological/green rather than a clear left or right populist ideology.

chunk 6 · The manifesto leans mildly left-populist: it calls for reducing social inequality, universal health coverage weighted by income, and criticises commercialisation of healthcare and private monopolies, while also invoking Orthodox cultural heritage and traditional identity, giving a weak right-cultural undertone.

chunk 7 · The text's limited populist framing leans slightly left-green, opposing corporate profit over public interest (tourism businesses vs. citizens), but lacks strong class-based or identity-based populist ideology; it is primarily an ecological/green manifesto.

Liberalism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto is strongly pro-liberal on political and social dimensions—championing direct democracy, rule of law, equality regardless of gender/sexuality/ethnicity, and media freedom—while being mixed-to-skeptical on economic liberalism (opposing TTIP's market-supremacy provisions) and mildly anti-liberal on financial markets (framing bank dependency as corrupt).

chunk 2 · The manifesto scores high on political liberalism (direct democracy, media freedom, anti-corruption, checks and balances) and social liberalism (elimination of discrimination against minorities, gender equality, women's rights), but is explicitly anti-liberal on economic and financial-market dimensions, rejecting the open-economy model, proposing a state bank, freezing non-performing loans, and framing international financial institutions as usurers.

chunk 3 · The manifesto supports democratic institutions, anti-racism, and capital-market transparency/independence, but explicitly rejects privatisation and favours state control of semi-public enterprises, yielding a mixed-to-moderate liberal profile across dimensions.

chunk 4 · The text supports democratic institutions, referenda, and anti-racism measures (pro-liberal), but also advocates strict immigration limits, state intervention in labour markets, and use of sovereign trust accounts to exit the bailout memorandum, producing a mixed liberal profile across dimensions.

chunk 5 · The manifesto supports democratic educational processes, respect for individual and cultural identity, and strengthening the Competition Protection Commission, reflecting moderate liberal values across political and economic dimensions, while financial-market policy is entirely absent.

chunk 6 · The manifesto supports gender equality in sport and transparency/accountability in governance (moderately pro-liberal politically and socially), but is sceptical of market-based healthcare, criticising its commercialisation and favouring a non-profit public system, which tempers economic liberalism; financial-market policy is entirely absent.

chunk 7 · The manifesto supports rule of law, rights of disabled persons, and fair pricing mechanisms, reflecting moderate liberal values in political and social dimensions, while economic liberalism is mixed due to calls for regulation and state intervention in environmental management.
