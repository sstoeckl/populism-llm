WarningHow to read this page

The scores are holistic judgements. Each model read the whole manifesto text (in chunks of up to 20,000 tokens) and rated every dimension from the text as a whole. The passages quoted further down are examples the model cited, not the basis of the score: a score that rests on two paragraphs cannot be reduced to a few words, and a quoted passage alone will often look too weak, or too strong, for the number next to it. Read the models’ own explanations first; they state the reasoning behind each block of scores. The quotes then show where in the text the models looked.

Populism, populist ideology and liberalism

Thirteen dimensions scored on 0–10 by three model families from the full manifesto text, each with a verbatim evidence quote. Scores are aggregated as in Rode & Stöckl (2026): a chunk counts if the model marks its evidence as verified and the quote is found verbatim in the manifesto chunk (and is not copied from the prompt); confidence-weighted mean within model, plain mean across models.

Populism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto consistently frames politics as a struggle between "the people" and corrupt elites/powerful economic forces, calling for a constituent process to return power to citizens and denouncing neoliberal policies that serve "los poderosos" at the expense of ordinary people.

chunk 2 · The manifesto frames financial capital and large corporations as predatory elites exploiting public goods (pensions, rescued banks), uses vivid anti-capitalist imagery ("garras capitalistas"), and positions the state as guarantor of the people's rights against these interests, yielding moderate-to-strong populist framing.

chunk 3 · The text frames the political-economic struggle as one between the corrupt capitalist elite/oligarchy and the majority social interest, invoking anti-elite rhetoric against "las élites que gobiernan esta Europa del capital" and the "oligarquía mediática y empresarial," while positioning the party as champion of "la mayoría social."

chunk 4 · The manifesto frames the judiciary and economic system as serving elite and capitalist minority interests against ordinary people, invoking people's sovereignty and condemning elitist access structures, though the tone is programmatic rather than strongly Manichean.

chunk 5 · The manifesto frames energy oligopolies and large multinationals as corrupt enemies of the people, invoking the citizenry as a unified virtuous group deserving democratic access to information, culture, and energy, with moderate but consistent anti-elite and people-centric rhetoric.

chunk 6 · The text shows moderate populist features: local elites ("caciques") are criticised for self-serving use of public funds, and participatory democracy for "the people" is championed, but the discourse is primarily programmatic and policy-oriented rather than strongly Manichean or people-centric in a populist sense.

chunk 7 · The manifesto frames the PP and PSOE as corrupt elites imposing neoliberal austerity on 'the people' and workers, while calling for socialist transformation, but the populist framing is secondary to detailed LGBTQI+ and social policy proposals.

chunk 8 · The text frames the state as serving the powerful at the expense of youth, invokes youth as a unified virtuous people denied voice, and casts the struggle as one between exploitative elites (speculators, vulture funds, the capitalist system) and ordinary young people.

Populist ideology

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The populism is firmly left-wing: the manifesto explicitly attacks neoliberalism, corporate power, and financial elites, advocates redistribution, workers' rights, public services, and a social republic, while explicitly condemning right-wing nationalist and fascist forces.

chunk 2 · The manifesto is strongly left-populist: it calls for nationalization of strategic sectors, public banking, redistribution through progressive taxation, guaranteed employment, and explicitly frames itself as incompatible with neoliberalism, with virtually no right-wing or nativist content.

chunk 3 · The populist ideology is clearly left-wing, centered on anti-neoliberalism, the capital/labor contradiction, redistribution, public ownership of banks, state intervention in the economy, and social equality, with no meaningful right-wing or nativist content.

chunk 4 · The manifesto is clearly left-populist, advocating public ownership, reversing privatizations, anti-capitalist framing, redistribution, and social equality, with no right-wing nativist or identity-protectionist content.

chunk 5 · The text is clearly left-populist: it advocates nationalization of the energy sector, public management of cultural rights, opposition to neoliberal market logic, redistribution through public services, and protection of workers and vulnerable groups against corporate elites.

chunk 6 · The text is clearly left-populist: it explicitly rejects the "economicista y liberal" agricultural model, calls for anti-capitalist, anti-imperialist feminism, opposes free-trade agreements (TTIP, CETA), and advocates redistribution and social equality, with no right-wing nationalist or identity-protectionist content.

chunk 7 · The text explicitly invokes socialism, anti-capitalism, class struggle, redistribution, and solidarity with left-wing Latin American governments, placing it firmly in the left-populist tradition.

chunk 8 · The manifesto explicitly calls for "Socialismo del siglo XXI" as the alternative to capitalism, advocates redistribution, public enterprise, labour regulation, and anti-corporate measures, placing it firmly on the left-populist spectrum with no right-wing or nativist elements.

Liberalism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto is pro-liberal on political democracy and social rights (participatory democracy, gender equality, LGBTQ+ recognition), but strongly anti-liberal economically (banning temp agencies, heavy labour market regulation, state planning) and in financial markets (restrictions on banks, capital controls on tax havens).

chunk 2 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal in the classical sense: it advocates nationalization of banks and strategic industries, a financial transactions tax, state-directed economic planning, heavy progressive taxation, and public monopolies in energy and finance, while showing only limited support for democratic institutions and gender equality.

chunk 3 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal in the classical/ordo-liberal sense: it explicitly calls for the state to "reequilibrate" (not merely correct) the market, advocates permanent state ownership of intervened banks, and promotes extensive public provision across education, health, and research, while showing moderate support for democratic participation and social rights.

chunk 4 · The manifesto scores high on social liberalism (reproductive rights, gender equality, minority protections) but is anti-liberal economically and financially, advocating state pharmaceutical industry, rent controls, expropriation of vacant housing, and hostility to financial market actors like vulture funds.

chunk 5 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal economically, opposing market-based energy pricing, advocating nationalization and public management of water, energy, and culture, while showing moderate support for political democracy and social inclusion of minorities; financial market liberalism is minimal.

chunk 6 · The text scores high on social liberalism (strong LGBTI and gender-equality rights), moderately on political liberalism (democracy and rights framing), but low on economic and financial-market liberalism, explicitly rejecting free-trade agreements, calling for a public bank, market regulation in agriculture, and opposing "neoliberal" policies.

chunk 7 · The manifesto is strongly pro-liberal on social/civil rights (extensive LGBTQI+ protections), moderately pro-liberal on political rights (democratic participation), but anti-liberal on economic and financial dimensions, opposing free trade agreements (TTIP), speculative finance, and multinational corporate power.

chunk 8 · The text supports expanded political participation (voting at 16) and social pluralism (gender, ethnic, sexual diversity), but is strongly anti-liberal economically—calling for de-marketisation of universities, elimination of private schools, public digital enterprise, and condemning speculators and vulture funds in housing.
