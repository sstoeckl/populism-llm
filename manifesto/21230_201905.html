WarningHow to read this page

The scores are holistic judgements. Each model read the whole manifesto text (in chunks of up to 20,000 tokens) and rated every dimension from the text as a whole. The passages quoted further down are examples the model cited, not the basis of the score: a score that rests on two paragraphs cannot be reduced to a few words, and a quoted passage alone will often look too weak, or too strong, for the number next to it. Read the models’ own explanations first; they state the reasoning behind each block of scores. The quotes then show where in the text the models looked.

Thirteen dimensions scored on 0–10 by three model families from the full manifesto text, each with a verbatim evidence quote. Scores are aggregated as in Rode & Stöckl (2026): a chunk counts if the model marks its evidence as verified and the quote is found verbatim in the manifesto chunk (and is not copied from the prompt); confidence-weighted mean within model, plain mean across models.

Populism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto consistently frames workers as a virtuous collective betrayed by corrupt elites (government, shareholders, platform capitalists), using terms like "Graaikapitalisme" and "Kiezersbedrog," and portrays politics as a moral struggle between ordinary workers and exploitative elites.

chunk 2 · The manifesto (PVDA/PTB, Belgium 2019) frames corporate energy multinationals and employers as exploiters of ordinary people, calls for public ownership "in handen van de samenleving," and portrays the market system as the root cause of climate and poverty crises, creating a moderate but consistent people-vs.-elite narrative.

chunk 3 · The manifesto frames large corporations and political elites as prioritising short-term profit over planetary survival, invokes the 'asbestmaffia' as a corrupt elite, and calls for state intervention to overcome market failures, reflecting moderate populist anti-elitism and a Manichean framing of industry vs. people.

chunk 4 · The text repeatedly frames capitalist agro-industry, property speculators, and profit-driven multinationals as corrupt forces harming ordinary people (farmers, tenants, elderly, disabled), invoking a moral good-vs-evil struggle between the people's needs and elite profit motives, while calling for collective public solutions.

chunk 5 · The manifesto consistently frames large corporations, banks, and the political establishment as corrupt or self-serving adversaries of the people, invoking the public/community as the rightful beneficiary of policy, with a clear good-vs-evil framing around private profit versus public interest.

chunk 6 · The text exhibits very high populism across all three sub-dimensions: it frames a corrupt "politieke kaste" and financial elite as enemies of the ordinary people, invokes "het volk" as a unified virtuous group deserving direct democratic power, and constructs a stark Manichean struggle between workers/small businesses and multinationals/rich shareholders.

chunk 7 · The text consistently frames the political establishment and big business as corrupt forces that have "confiscated" democracy, invokes "the people" as the rightful sovereign through participatory democracy proposals, and draws a sharp moral contrast between ordinary working people and elites (corporations, N-VA, government), scoring high on all three populism sub-dimensions.

chunk 8 · The text frames justice as serving the establishment over ordinary citizens, attacks named political opponents (De Wever) as responsible for failed policies, and invokes the people's basic rights against corrupt elites, scoring moderately on populism across all three sub-dimensions.

chunk 9 · The manifesto consistently frames European institutions as captured by corporate elites ("draaideuren tussen de Europese instellingen en het grootbedrijf"), portrays the people as the legitimate sovereign ("We laten de mensen beslissen"), and constructs a Manichean struggle between a corrupt establishment coalition and ordinary workers/citizens ("kwarteeuw sociale afbraak").

Populist ideology

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The populist appeals are firmly left-wing: the manifesto calls for redistribution of wealth from shareholders to workers, higher minimum wages, stronger unions, public healthcare, and frames inequality as a structural result of capitalism and neoliberal government policy.

chunk 2 · The manifesto is strongly left-populist: it explicitly names capitalism as the structural cause of poverty and climate crisis, calls for public ownership of energy, redistribution of wealth, and social rights for vulnerable groups, with no right-wing or nativist elements.

chunk 3 · The manifesto is clearly left-populist: it attacks capitalist agro-industry, financial speculation, and corporate lobbying, advocates public ownership, redistribution, and worker/farmer rights, with no nationalist or right-wing identity content.

chunk 4 · The manifesto is clearly left-populist: it advocates redistribution through progressive taxation, public ownership of housing and land, nationalization of care and banking, anti-corporate rhetoric targeting capitalist agro-industry and real-estate speculators, and strong social protections — all hallmarks of left-wing populism with no right-wing identity or nativist elements.

chunk 5 · The populist ideology is firmly left-wing, centered on redistribution (miljonairstaks), public ownership of banks, anti-corporate critique, social investment, and a RedGreen Deal that explicitly rejects neoliberal austerity and champions workers and the poor against capital.

chunk 6 · The populism is overwhelmingly left-wing: the manifesto calls for a millionaire's wealth tax, redistribution from large corporations to workers and SMEs, nationalization-adjacent public banking, Tobin tax on financial transactions, and anti-corporate platform regulation, with virtually no right-wing nativist or identity-based populist content.

chunk 7 · The manifesto is strongly left-populist: it targets industrial and financial "mastodons," champions redistribution, workers' rights, trade union power, anti-racism, gender equality, and LGBT+ rights, while explicitly rejecting right-wing identity politics and anti-immigration framing.

chunk 8 · The manifesto is clearly left-populist: it emphasizes social inequality as the root of crime and addiction, champions redistribution, public services, workers' rights, and LGBT+ minority protections, while explicitly opposing neoliberal competition and privatization.

chunk 9 · The manifesto is strongly left-populist: it calls for banning hedge funds, imposing a Tobin tax, expanding public services, redistributing wealth via corporate taxes, and explicitly frames the struggle as workers/people versus multinationals and financial elites, with no right-wing nationalist or centrist populist content.

Liberalism

Sonnet

chunk 1 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal across economic and financial dimensions, advocating state control over healthcare, drug pricing, and credit markets, while showing limited support for anti-discrimination norms (lib_social) and expressing concern about undemocratic government decisions (lib_political).

chunk 2 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal economically—explicitly rejecting market liberalisation of energy, calling for public ownership, price controls, and strict credit-sector regulation—while showing moderate support for democratic accountability and anti-discrimination norms.

chunk 3 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal economically: it advocates renationalisation of rail and water, reversal of liberalisation, price controls, bans on speculation, and state-directed investment, while offering limited support for democratic transparency and social rights.

chunk 4 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal in the classical sense: it advocates state-set minimum prices for agricultural products, public banks, rent caps, nationalization of land and housing, heavy regulation of markets, and explicitly frames market liberalization as harmful — while supporting social inclusion and rights for disabled persons, which is the one area with a more liberal stance.

chunk 5 · The manifesto is broadly anti-liberal economically and financially, advocating state banks, public investment, nationalization of Belfius, bans on hedge fund lending, and rejection of market logic in care and culture; it scores higher on political and social liberalism through support for participatory democracy and anti-discrimination measures.

chunk 6 · The text is mixed on liberalism: it supports political rights, civil liberties, and participatory democracy (moderately pro-liberal), but is strongly anti-liberal on economic and financial-market dimensions, advocating wealth taxes, Tobin taxes, bank profit taxes, bans on tax havens, and state-directed lending over market mechanisms.

chunk 7 · The text is strongly pro-liberal on social dimensions (universal human rights, LGBT+ equality, anti-racism, women's rights) and on participatory political rights, but explicitly anti-liberal on economic and financial-market dimensions, opposing labour-market flexibility, corporate power, and market-based childcare, yielding a mixed overall liberalism score.

chunk 8 · The text scores high on lib_social (strong LGBT+ rights advocacy) and lib_political (rule of law, democratic oversight), but scores low on lib_economic and lib_financial_market due to explicit opposition to liberalization, privatization, capital flows, and calls for Tobin taxes and bans on financial instruments.

chunk 9 · The manifesto is strongly anti-liberal economically and financially (banning hedge funds, derivatives, short selling, capital controls, nationalization of public services, 25% corporate tax floor), while showing mixed political liberalism (pro-democracy and privacy rights but anti-EU institutional checks) and moderate social liberalism (defending refugee and minority rights).
